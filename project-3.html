Lunar Rover Design

A SolidWorks CAD project completed at RMIT University. I designed the wheels for a six-wheeled lunar rover, working in a multidisciplinary engineering team of six.

Overview

As part of RMIT University’s OENG1250 Introduction to Engineering Design course, our team was tasked with designing a lunar rover capable of collecting and delivering regolith on the Moon’s surface. The project was inspired by the Australian Moon to Mars initiative — a real-world collaboration between Australian universities, industry, and NASA. Six of us each took ownership of one rover component, designing it from scratch in SolidWorks and integrating it into a full group assembly.

My component was the wheels. I was responsible for designing a set of six wheels that were lightweight, durable, and capable of traversing loose and abrasive lunar regolith — while also interfacing correctly with the drive motor being designed by my teammate.

My Design

The wheel had to sit within a mandated diameter range of 80–130 mm. Through speed calculations using the motor’s 10 RPM output, I determined that a 130 mm diameter was necessary to get close to the required speed band of 0.28–0.37 km/h, with a small gear-up ratio of 1.14–1.51x needed to bridge the gap.

  • Tread pattern — after comparing a chevron/arrow pattern against a wavy sine pattern in CAD, I selected the chevron design for its superior self-cleaning ability and forward traction on loose regolith, backed by NASA Apollo rover research
  • Material — compared aluminium, steel, titanium, and carbon fibre; selected Aluminium 6061-T6 at 116g per wheel for its balance of weight, durability, and aerospace-proven abrasion resistance when hard-anodised
  • Dimensions — 130 mm diameter × 35 mm wide, with a 22 mm boss, 6 mm bore for the motor shaft, and six spokes at 3 mm thick × 10 mm wide
  • Sub-assembly — integrated the wheel with the drive motor and gearbox housing into a compact, independently-powered drive module
  • Ground pressure check — calculated a contact pressure of 10.3 kPa, confirming the wheel would not sink excessively into loose regolith

The Process

  • Sketching & ideation — hand-sketched initial wheel geometries and tread patterns before moving into CAD
  • Preliminary CAD model — built in SolidWorks and shared with the team by Week 6 to allow sub-assembly testing with adjacent components
  • Detailed design — refined geometry, completed technical drawings with orthographic, section, and isometric views, and produced a full bill of materials
  • Team meetings — attended 6 meetings across the project to coordinate interface dimensions and resolve sub-assembly issues as they came up

What I Learned

This was my first time going through a full engineering design process — from a hand sketch to a dimensioned CAD model to a proper technical drawing. Having to justify every decision (why 130 mm, why aluminium, why chevron) through calculations and research made me think about design in a much more structured way than I had before.

Working across a team of six where every component had to physically connect to someone else’s work was also a completely different challenge. You can’t just design in isolation — your dimensions affect everyone around you, and that coordination is its own skill.

  • SolidWorks — built preliminary and detailed models, technical drawings, and a sub-assembly to industry drawing standards
  • Engineering calculations — applied speed, gear ratio, mass, and ground pressure calculations to justify every design decision
  • Material selection — learned how to compare materials systematically against real engineering constraints
  • Team coordination — managed shared interface dimensions across six different workstreams, adapting my design when sub-assembly issues came up
